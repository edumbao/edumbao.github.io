I Am Building a Small Lead-Generation SaaS for Cleaning Businesses

Published on November 21, 2026

I am building CleanServiceLeads, a small SaaS for commercial cleaning and janitorial owners. It helps them find local business prospects, score which ones are worth calling first, and keep follow-up in one place. I am learning the architecture, debugging, and deployment as I go, by shipping the work in small, review-only steps.

The problem

A cleaning business that still does its own prospecting has a slow first step. Someone has to build a call list for a city, copy public contact details, and decide who to dial first. That work takes time, and a long unranked list makes it easy to call weaker prospects before stronger ones.

The product is aimed at US commercial cleaning and janitorial owners who look for offices, clinics, gyms, property managers, and similar local businesses in their service area.

What I built

The app has a public marketing site and a signed-in dashboard. You search by city and business type, see how many matches exist, and get a preview list with contact details hidden. Each lead gets a score from 0 to 100 and a Hot, Warm, or Low label.

The score adds points for signals already on the public listing, such as a website, phone, email, a person contact, rating, reviews, and address. Hot means the score is 75 or higher. It does not mean the business is hiring a cleaner, and it does not mean an email is on file.

When you choose a prospect, you unlock the contact details that are on file and save that lead to a pipeline. The statuses are Saved, Contacted, Follow Up, Ready, and Not a Fit. You can add notes. Paid plans can export the pipeline to CSV. Re-opening a saved lead does not use another unlock. There is a free plan with limited unlocks, and paid plans when a pipeline needs more capacity. Checkout runs through a merchant of record.

Around the product I built a public guides library. As of 21 November 2026, nine guides are live, grouped into finding leads, deciding who to call first, reaching the decision maker, and comparing tools. I write some myself. The others go out under the product team byline.

The stack is Next.js with the App Router, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS, with Clerk for sign-in, Postgres on Neon through Prisma, and Vercel for hosting. Public listing data is fetched on the server, not in the browser. I started the repository at the end of May 2026 as a local dashboard, then added authentication, a real database for saved leads, plan limits, billing, SEO, and the guides.

How I built it

I am a technical writer who builds software hands-on. I am not a senior engineer. I needed a way to move this product forward without pretending I could hold the whole system in my head at once.

I run a small AI agent team the same way I already run content work. There is a chief-of-staff agent, a developer agent, a content agent, and an SEO agent. I write the brief. They do one assigned job. I review the result. Nothing is merged, published, or billed without my yes. Pull requests stay review-only until I approve them. I check the diff, the tests, and the claims in the public copy. When a guide needs a featured image, I make it and upload it myself, then I merge.

The office cleaning leads guide is one example. I merged that review-only pull request on 17 November 2026, after I had the featured image in place. The next day I merged a second review-only pull request that tightened SEO across the eight guides then live, and had the production pages checked.

What broke and what I fixed

Public copy can drift away from the code. The score is additive. A complete listing with no email and no person contact scores 70, which is Warm. An email adds 20 points. A person contact adds 10. A lead can reach Hot without an email if a person contact is present on an otherwise full listing. Early marketing pages did not describe that clearly. In one version, the advertised signals added up to 70 while the same page said Hot started at 75, which made "Hot" sound impossible. I left the formula alone and rewrote the homepage, the how it works page, the sample list, and the FAQ so they match the math.

A search returned zero results, and it was easy to assume the data API was down. The city name had been typed wrong. The integration was fine. I still treat empty results as a prompt to check the input first, then the wiring.

By mid-November, eight guides were live, but the search titles were too long (one ran to 102 characters), some sections overlapped, vendor prices were repeated on more than one page, and a few guides reused the homepage image in their social previews. The SEO pull request rewrote titles and meta descriptions to fit search-snippet limits, moved repeated price lists to the comparison guide, added related-guide blocks and internal links, and gave each guide its own image, byline dates, and structured data. No live guide URL was changed.

A later pre-release review led to a hardening pass. I pinned the Next.js version instead of leaving it on a floating tag, added stricter browser security headers, closed a path that could save preview results without an unlock, and stopped sending internal error details to the browser.

What I learned

The useful unit of work, for me, is a small pull request with a written brief and a pass/fail list.

A product site and its guides are part of the same system. If the scoring page, the FAQ, and the code disagree, the reader is right to stop trusting the tool. I now treat "does this sentence still match the code?" as part of review, not as later polish. The same rule applies to the guides: one main keyword per article, links instead of copied sections, and no URL changes once a page is live.

I am still learning how the pieces fit: auth, a hosted database, webhooks, search metering, and deployments. The agents can draft the change. I still have to understand enough to accept or reject it.

What's next

I am continuing the guides. The latest one, published on 21 November 2026, is a practical follow-up plan for a commercial cleaning quote. New guides will keep the same rules: one keyword, related links both ways, and a human yes before anything goes live. I will also keep reviewing the product the way I review a draft, and fix what is off before I add scope.
